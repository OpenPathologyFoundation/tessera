# Literature Review: Manual White‑Blood‑Cell Differential Counting and Application Design Considerations

## Introduction

Manual white‑blood‑cell (WBC) differentials remain an important tool for confirming automated analyzer results and for counting cells that automated instruments flag as abnormal. However, the practice varies widely among laboratories. The purpose of this review is to collect high‑profile literature that informs development of the WBC ΔΣ manual differential counting application. Key themes include cell categories used in peripheral‑blood and bone‑marrow differentials, the number of cells counted, the reliability of band neutrophil counts, reference ranges for WBCs, and the advantages and limitations of manual versus automated or digital methods.

---

## Peripheral‑Blood Manual Differential

### Cell categories and workflow

- **Peripheral‑blood smear preparation** — Classical manuals describe spreading a drop of blood on a slide, staining it with a Romanowski/May–Grünwald–Giemsa stain, and counting/identifying cells under high‑power microscopy. Clinical textbooks recommend counting 200 WBCs and classifying them into neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils, with additional categories (e.g., bands, myelocytes, blasts) when immature cells are present.[^1]

- **Hospital SOPs** — Many clinical laboratories count 100 cells for routine samples, increase to 200 cells if the total WBC is high (>15×10³/µL) or if immature cells are present, and count fewer than 100 cells when WBCs are very low.[^2] Cells not seen are reported as zero; percentages are extrapolated to 100 and reported with the number counted.[^2]

- **Reference method (CLSI H20‑A2)** — The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) defines a reference leukocyte differential count in which two qualified examiners each perform a 200‑cell differential on two slides (total 400 cells) and data are recorded per slide and examiner.[^3] Band neutrophils are added to the total neutrophils for statistical analysis when the instrument does not report bands separately.[^3]

- **Flow‑cytometric reference method** — A 2025/2026 International Council for Standardization in Haematology (ICSH) study proposes a flow‑cytometric WBC reference method. This method acknowledges that the current manual reference (CLSI H20‑A2) counts 200 cells per reviewer and suffers from dependence on expertise, smear quality and non‑uniform cell distribution.[^4] The flow‑cytometric method counts large numbers of nucleated cells and demonstrates equivalent or better performance for rare cell populations; it could eventually replace manual morphology for instrument validation.[^4]

### Reliability of band neutrophil counts

- **Poor reproducibility** — CAP proficiency‑testing articles note that band neutrophil counts are imprecise. Reproducibility declines as the number of technologists increases, and the greater the number performing differentials, the greater the expected variability.[^5] Reference ranges for bands vary widely (upper limits 5–20%).[^5]

- **CLSI/ICSH recommendations** — The 2015 International Council for Standardization in Haematology (ICSH) guidelines for peripheral blood morphology recommend counting band neutrophils as segmented neutrophils because separate identification of bands lacks inter‑observer agreement and is not routinely reported.[^6] CAP proficiency‑testing reports show that grouping bands with neutrophils improves precision; the CAP committee recommends eliminating bands as a separate category.[^5]

- **Studies of inter‑observer variability** — A 2024 Croatian multicentric study found high coefficients of variation for band neutrophil counts (15.4–62.4%) and concluded that manual band counting is highly variable; counting bands with neutrophils is recommended.[^7]

### Reference values and normal WBC proportions

StatPearls notes that in adults, neutrophils constitute 40–60% of WBCs, lymphocytes 20–40%, monocytes 2–8%, eosinophils 1–4% and basophils 0.5–1%.[^8] These ranges provide a general reference for peripheral‑blood differentials. Neutrophilia is defined as >7,700 neutrophils/µL.[^8] Variation in normal values and influences of age, pregnancy and other factors are discussed in the same source.[^8]

### Manual count imprecision and sample size

- **Statistical error** — Manual peripheral‑blood differentials typically count 100–200 cells, leading to statistical variation; early automated instruments that counted 100–200 cells had similar imprecision.[^9]

- **Buffy‑coat and low‑count samples** — Some laboratories perform truncated manual differentials (25–50 cells) on very low WBC counts, but such counts carry substantial statistical imprecision.[^2] Automated differentials are generally more precise and should be used whenever the instrument flags are acceptable.[^2]

- **Digital morphology analyzers** — Comparative studies of the Sysmex DI‑60 digital morphology analyzer show that manual WBC differential counts remain the reference standard but are time‑consuming and risk‑prone. Manual counts follow CLSI H20‑A2: two experts count 200 cells per slide, and the manual result is the mean of their counts.[^10] The DI‑60 counts pre‑classified cells per slide and includes 18 WBC types, but manual confirmation is still required.[^10] Failure mode and effect analysis (FMEA) demonstrated a five‑fold lower risk with the DI‑60 than with manual counting, yet manual counting had shorter turnaround time except in severe leukopenia.[^10]

### Manual smear review criteria

The International Consensus Group for Hematology Review developed 41 criteria to trigger manual smear review after automated CBC; they observed little uniformity among laboratories in criteria and emphasized the need for standardisation.[^11] The guidelines help laboratories decide *when* to perform manual differentials, rather than defining the counting procedure itself.

## Bone‑Marrow Differential Counts

### Cell composition and counting procedure

- **Scope** — Bone‑marrow differentials classify myeloid and monocytic cells, erythroid progenitors, lymphoid elements and plasma cells, excluding megakaryocytes and non‑hematopoietic cells.[^12] Erythroid and granulopoietic cells are intermingled in normal marrow; the myeloid/erythroid (M:E) ratio is normally 2:1–4:1 (or 1.5–3:1 when segmented neutrophils are excluded).[^12]

- **Number of cells counted** — The College of American Pathologists (CAP) recommends counting 500 cells in a bone‑marrow aspirate differential,[^12] but several studies question the necessity of 500 cells:

    - A 2018 study comparing counts on 165 cases found no statistical differences between 300‑ and 500‑cell counts and concluded that counting 300 cells provides clinically and statistically equivalent results.[^13]

    - A 2023 prospective study comparing 200‑ and 500‑cell differentials concluded that a 200‑cell differential is sufficient for most clinical applications; a 500‑cell count was needed in only ~1% of cases when results were near a diagnostic cutoff.[^14]

    - StatPearls acknowledges the CAP recommendation but notes that research supports 300‑cell differentials.[^12]

- **Reference ranges and variability** — A 2020 *Annals of Hematology* study established reference ranges in 236 healthy donors and found wide variability in percentages of erythroblasts (8.5–56.5%), granulocytes (24.5–72.5%) and other cell types.[^15] Differences were observed between sexes and across studies, likely due to small sample sizes and subjective classification.[^15]

### Manual versus automated methods

- **Imprecision** — Manual bone‑marrow differentials are labour‑intensive and imprecise due to counting a small fraction (<500) of cells.[^13] Location on the slide and subjective identification contribute to variability.[^15] Automated digital or flow‑cytometric methods sample larger numbers of cells and improve precision.[^4]

- **Digital morphology analyzers** — The Sysmex DI‑60 provides 18 pre‑classified WBC categories, including segmented and band neutrophils, myelocytes, promyelocytes, atypical lymphocytes and plasma cells; it still requires expert verification.[^10]

- **Flow cytometry** — The proposed ICSH WBC reference method identifies standard five populations (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils) plus immature granulocytes, blasts and nucleated red cells using antigenic markers.[^4] It reduces dependence on morphological expertise and improves enumeration of infrequent populations.[^4]

### Configuration of cell categories

Because bone‑marrow differentials include precursors absent from peripheral blood, laboratories use different cell categories and may group certain precursors together. A 2024 dataset for machine‑learning classification followed CLSI guidelines and included blast, myelocyte, metamyelocyte, band neutrophil, segmented neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, eosinophil, basophil, neoplastic lymphocyte, reactive lymphocyte, atypical lymphocyte and nucleated red cell; the authors grouped band neutrophil and segmented neutrophil as "neutrophil" and combined several rare cells into an "other" category for analysis.[^16] This demonstrates that cell categories can be reconfigured without loss of diagnostic utility.

## Band Neutrophils and Derived Parameters

### Clinical utility of band counts

- **Cornbleet review (2002)** — A widely cited review concluded that band counts are nonspecific, inaccurate and imprecise, provide little diagnostic support in patients older than three months, and that WBC counts and absolute neutrophil counts are better tests.[^17] Band counts have variable sensitivity and specificity for neonatal sepsis and may be replaced by newer biomarkers in the future.[^17]

- **CAP proficiency testing (2024)** — Survey results showed that 86% of laboratories still report band counts, but classification of moderate and difficult bands is poor.[^5] This highlights the need for standardization.

### Derived parameters and formulas

- **Myeloid‑erythroid (M:E) ratio** — The M:E ratio compares granulocytic to erythroid precursors. Reference values range from 1.5–3:1 when segmented neutrophils are excluded and 2–4:1 when they are included.[^12] The ratio must be interpreted with peripheral‑blood findings; elevated ratios suggest myeloid hyperplasia or erythroid hypoplasia, while low ratios suggest erythroid hyperplasia or myeloid hypoplasia.

- **Interpretation of abnormal cells** — Some laboratories monitor immature granulocyte counts (myelocytes + metamyelocytes + promyelocytes) and blast percentages. The CAP/ICSH guidelines recommend using instrument‑derived absolute neutrophil counts and immature granulocyte counts rather than manual band counts for assessing infections or myeloid disorders.[^6]

## Manual Differential Counts in Body Fluids

Although WBC ΔΣ focuses on blood and marrow, guidelines for other body fluids illustrate manual counting principles. The CLSI H56‑A guideline for body‑fluid analysis was followed in a 2022 study comparing automated and manual analyses of pleural effusion. Manual cytospin analysis counted at least 200 cells at 400× magnification and classified leukocytes into neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes/macrophages, eosinophils and basophils.[^18] Manual cytospin is considered the gold standard but suffers from imprecision, high inter‑observer variability and long turnaround time.[^18] Automated analysis correlated well overall but showed etiology‑specific discrepancies.[^18]

---

## Implications for WBC ΔΣ Application Design

1. **Configurable cell categories and keyboard mapping** — Literature shows that laboratories differ in the cell categories they use (e.g., grouping bands with neutrophils, collapsing rare precursors into an "other" category).[^6][^16] The application should allow users to load a configuration file (e.g., JSON) defining specimen types, cell categories, keyboard shortcuts and output templates. By default, the app should group bands with neutrophils and offer the option to enable a separate band category for labs that insist on reporting bands.

2. **Customizable cell count targets** — CAP recommends a 500‑cell bone‑marrow differential, but evidence shows 300‑cell or even 200‑cell counts provide equivalent clinical information.[^13][^14] The application should allow laboratories to set the minimum cell count per specimen type and enforce this threshold before "Count Done" to satisfy regulatory requirements.

3. **Incorporate derived formulas** — M:E ratio and other derived parameters should be calculated automatically based on the counts. Users should be able to define formulas in the configuration file. The application should display formulas with at least one decimal place and ensure percentages sum to 100%.[^12]

4. **Support for comments and morphological notes** — Many guidelines emphasise morphological observations, such as dysplasia, toxic granulation or blasts. The application must include a free‑text comments field and incorporate comments into the report.[^6]

5. **Session history and export** — Because manual differentials are often used for multiple cases during a session, the system should retain completed counts locally during the browser session and allow export to CSV/JSON. Data should not persist after the session to respect privacy.

6. **Guidance for operators** — Given the variability and imprecision of manual counts, the application should provide clear instructions: ensure good smear preparation, count from the thin edge of the smear, avoid overlapping cells and adjust cell counts per institutional protocols. It should also remind users that manual band counts are not recommended and encourage use of automated counts when available.[^17]

---

## Conclusion

The literature demonstrates that manual differential counting is still widely used but suffers from variability, imprecision and lack of standardisation. High‑profile studies and guidelines recommend:

- Counting 100–200 cells for peripheral‑blood differentials and 200–500 cells for bone‑marrow differentials, with evidence supporting reduced cell counts without compromising clinical interpretation.[^3][^13][^14]
- Grouping band neutrophils with segmented neutrophils because separate band counts are unreliable.[^6][^17]
- Recognising that reference ranges for bone‑marrow differentials are broad and influenced by sex, body composition and subjective classification, necessitating flexibility in reporting.[^15]
- Using automated or digital methods wherever possible due to their ability to count thousands of cells and reduce bias.[^4][^10]

These findings support the design of a configurable, user‑friendly manual differential application that adapts to diverse laboratory practices while promoting evidence‑based standards.
